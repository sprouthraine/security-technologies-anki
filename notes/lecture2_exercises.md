## Section A – Atomic Structure

### Q1. Describe the Bohr model of the atom. Why is it called a "planetary" model? [3]
**Solution**
- Negatively charged electrons orbit a central nucleus of positive protons and neutral neutrons (nucleons). Equal numbers of protons and electrons give a net charge of zero.
- "Planetary": the electrons are like planets orbiting the sun (nucleus). The **electrostatic force** plays the role of **gravity**.

### Q2. How many electrons can the n = 4 shell hold? Give the ground-state electron arrangement of sodium (Z = 11) and explain its reactivity. [3]
**Solution**
- 2n² = 2 × 16 = **32 electrons**
- Na: **2, 8, 1**
- It has 1 valence electron, which is easily lost to form Na⁺. So it is a highly reactive **Group 1 alkali metal**.

### Q3. Give the charge (in C) and mass (in kg) of (a) a proton and (b) an alpha particle. [4]
**Solution**
- (a) Proton: +1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ C; 1 mᵤ = 1.661 × 10⁻²⁷ kg
- (b) Alpha: +2e = **3.204 × 10⁻¹⁹ C**; 4 mᵤ = **6.644 × 10⁻²⁷ kg**

## Section B – Nuclide Notation, Isotopes & Ions

### Q4. For ²³⁵₉₂U, state the number of protons, neutrons and electrons (neutral atom). [3]
**Solution:** p = 92; n = 235 − 92 = **143**; e = 92

### Q5. What is an isotope? How many neutrons are in carbon-12, carbon-13 and carbon-14? [3]
**Solution:** Isotopes have the same atomic number Z (same element) but a different number of neutrons N, so a different A. C-12 has **6**, C-13 has **7**, C-14 has **8**.

## Section C – The Periodic Table

### Q7. Describe the properties of (a) alkali metals, (b) transition metals, (c) metalloids, (d) noble gases. [4]
**Solution**
- (a) **Group 1:** 1 valence electron, highly reactive (more so down the group), soft, tarnish in air, stored in oil.
- (b) **Groups 3–12:** good conductors of heat and electricity, malleable, high density and melting point (except Hg), less reactive. Examples: Fe, Cu, Ag, Au.
- (c) On the metal/non-metal boundary, with properties of both. **Semiconductors** (Si, Ge) used in electronics.
- (d) **Group 18:** full outer shells, so unreactive, monatomic, colourless and odourless. Example: argon in light bulbs prevents filament oxidation.

### Q8. Why do H and He not fit neatly into a group? [1]
**Solution:** Their electron configurations and properties do not match any group comfortably. H is loosely placed in Group 1 and He in Group 18 by closest properties.

## Section D – Mass Spectrometry

### Q9. Describe the three stages of a Time-of-Flight mass spectrometer. [5]
**Solution**
1. **Ionisation:** the sample is converted into positive ions (electron, chemical or electrospray ionisation).
2. **Acceleration / mass analysis:** an electric field accelerates the ions. **Lighter ions (for the same charge) travel faster**, separating them by m/z.
3. **Detection:** the ions trigger a current at the detector and the flight time is measured. Current strength indicates **abundance**.

### Q10. On a mass spectrograph, what do the peak height and x-axis position show? Give three security applications. [3]
**Solution**
- Height = **abundance**; x-position = **mass-to-charge ratio**. Each substance has a unique signature.
- **Applications:** drug detection (cocaine), explosive detection (TNT), blood-alcohol measurement, identifying radioactive material, forensic trace evidence.

### Q11. How does a magnetic sector spectrometer differ from ToF? [2]
**Solution:** It uses a **magnetic field** to bend ions along curved paths that depend on charge-to-mass ratio. The ions strike **different regions** of the detector, rather than being separated by flight time.

## Section E – Radioactivity

### Q12. Complete the table for α, β⁻, β⁺ and γ: composition, charge, mass, and the effect on Z and A. [8]
**Solution**
| | Composition | Charge | Mass | ΔZ | ΔA |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| α | 2p + 2n (He nucleus) | +2e | 4 mᵤ | −2 | −4 |
| β⁻ | electron from the nucleus (n → p) | −e | 1/1836 mᵤ | +1 | 0 |
| β⁺ | positron (p → n) | +e | 1/1836 mᵤ | −1 | 0 |
| γ | EM wave | 0 | 0 | 0 | 0 |

### Q13. Where does a β⁻ particle come from? [1]
**Solution:** From the **nucleus**, when a neutron changes into a proton. It does **not** come from the electron shells.

### Q14. Rank α, β and γ by (a) penetrating power and (b) ionising power, and state what stops each. [4]
**Solution**
- (a) Penetration: γ > β > α
- (b) Ionisation: α > β > γ
- **Stopped by:** α by paper or skin (a few cm in air); β by wood, plastic or thin metal (a few m in air); γ only by high-Z materials such as **lead**.

### Q15. Why could polonium-210 be smuggled through an airport, and why is it so deadly when ingested? [3]
**Solution**
- Po-210 is an **alpha emitter**. Alphas are stopped by **glass**, so a sealed container shows little external radiation.
- When ingested, alpha (the most strongly ionising radiation) damages DNA and cells directly, causing organ failure. It has a 30-day biological half-life, and the maximum safe dose is only 7 pg.

## Section F – Stability, Half-Life & Activity

### Q16. State the two rules for nuclear stability and explain why heavy nuclei need extra neutrons. [3]
**Solution**
- (i) N ≈ Z for Z < 20; (ii) N > Z for Z > 20.
- Extra neutrons add **strong-force attraction** and **separate the protons**, reducing the electrostatic repulsion between them.

### Q17. How do neutron-rich and proton-rich nuclides decay? [2]
**Solution**
- **Neutron-rich** (above the stability line): β⁻ (n → p).
- **Proton-rich** (below the line): β⁺ (lighter nuclides) or α (heavier nuclides).

### Q18. Define the becquerel and convert 7.4 × 10¹⁰ Bq into curies. [2]
**Solution:** 1 Bq = 1 disintegration per second (s⁻¹). 7.4 × 10¹⁰ / 3.7 × 10¹⁰ = **2 Ci**

### Q19. Po-210 has t½ = 138 days. What fraction of a sample remains after 414 days? [2]
**Solution:** 414/138 = 3 half-lives, so (½)³ = **1/8 (12.5%)**

### Q20. A sample contains 80 µg of carbon-14 (t½ = 5,730 yr). How much remains after 17,190 years? [2]
**Solution:** 17,190/5,730 = 3 half-lives, so 80 → 40 → 20 → **10 µg**

## Section G – Decay Equations

### Q21. Complete and balance these decay equations. [6]
(a) ²¹⁰₈₄Po → ? + ⁴₂He
(b) ²³⁹₉₄Pu → ? + ⁴₂He
(c) ¹⁴₆C → ? + ⁰₋₁e
(d) ⁹⁰₃₈Sr → ? + ⁰₋₁e
(e) ¹¹₆C → ? + ⁰₊₁e
(f) ²³⁸₉₂U → ? + ⁴₂He

**Solution**
- (a) **²⁰⁶₈₂Pb** (polonium decays to lead)
- (b) **²³⁵₉₂U**
- (c) **¹⁴₇N** (radiocarbon dating)
- (d) **⁹⁰₃₉Y**
- (e) **¹¹₅B**
- (f) **²³⁴₉₀Th**

### Q22. Thorium-228 (²²⁸₉₀Th) decays to lead-208 (²⁰⁸₈₂Pb) through a series of α and β⁻ decays. How many of each? [3]
**Solution**
- ΔA = 228 − 208 = 20. Only α changes A, so there are **5 α decays** (5 × 4 = 20).
- 5 α decays reduce Z by 10, from 90 to 80. The final Z is 82, so **2 β⁻ decays** (each +1).
- Answer: **5 α and 2 β⁻**.

## Section H – Case Study: Litvinenko

### Q23. Give three pieces of evidence that the Litvinenko poisoning was state-sponsored. [3]
**Solution**
- Po-210 is extremely rare: about 100 g a year is made in **nuclear reactors**.
- The amount used would cost **tens of millions of dollars**.
- The suspects (Lugovoy and Kovtun, ex-FSB links) are **protected from extradition by Russia**.
- The inquiry concluded the killing was "probably approved by Putin".

### Q24. Name two instruments used to detect and identify the radiation, and state how each works. [4]
**Solution**
- **Geiger counter:** detects ionising events in a Geiger–Müller tube and reads out counts per second (audible clicks).
- **Alpha-particle spectrometer:** identifies the isotope by the characteristic alpha energy (Po-210: **5.3 MeV**). It works similarly to a mass spectrometer.
