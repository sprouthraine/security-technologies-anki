# PART 1 – RADIO FREQUENCY IDENTIFICATION (RFID)

## 1. What Is RFID?
- **RFID = Radio Frequency Identification.**
- A technology that uses **radio-frequency electromagnetic fields** to **identify and track transponders (tags)** containing electronically stored information.
- Tags can be attached to **objects, animals or people**.
- A **two-way communication system** between a **reader** and a **tag**.

## 2. RFID in the Urban Landscape
| Application area | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Access control & person ID** | Biometric passports and border crossings; secure workplaces and universities; gyms, lockers; car and bike sharing (Zipcar) |
| **Transport flow monitoring** | New York's intelligent transport system (**E-ZPass**) |
| **Payment systems** | **Oyster card**, contactless payment (Android Pay) |
| **Loss prevention** | Retail item tagging |
| **Goods tracking** | Supply chain, animals |

## 3. Frequency Spectrum & Read Range
| Band | Frequency | Typical read range |
|---|---|---|
| **LF RFID** | 125 – 134 kHz | ~10 cm |
| **HF RFID** | 13.56 MHz | < 3 m |
| **UHF RFID** (latest technology) | 433 MHz; 865 – 960 MHz | up to 100 m |

## 4. History
- Originally developed to **replace barcodes** in supply chains. Advantages over barcodes:
  - **Wireless**
  - Works at **much further distances**
  - Holds **more information**
  - **More robust**
  - Allows **simultaneous reading** of many tags (e.g. a whole pallet)

## 5. RFID Tag Construction
- **Two key components:**
  1. **Microchip (IC):** a few electronic components such as a **capacitor, diode and transistor**
  2. **Antenna coil:** can be **printed**
- Some tags are only a few mm in size.
- Some contain a **battery** (active and semi-passive tags).

## 6. Global Proliferation
- The market was estimated to reach **USD 40.5 billion by 2025**.
- **Drivers:**
  - Falling production costs (cheaper ICs and components; **RFID printing**)
  - Companies building tags into goods at manufacture
  - In 2016, 50% of the world's top 100 retailers were working on RFID integration (stock tracking, loss prevention)
  - Growth of **smart cards** (travel, payment)
  - The need for **secure access control**, driven by growing cities and the terror threat

## 7. Privacy & Security Threats
| Attack | Description |
|---|---|
| **Unauthorised scanning (skimming / spying)** | Reading tags to profile a person: ID, medication (reveals illness), passport (biometric profile). Requires the encryption to be known or hacked |
| **Reprogramming** | Rewriting a tag, e.g. to make an expensive item cheaper in a shop. Requires the encryption to be known or hacked |
| **Replay attack** | **Intercept and record** the modulated backscatter signal (e.g. as someone enters a building), then **replay** it to the reader later |
| **Denial of Service (DoS) / jamming** | RFID frequencies are well known, so an **interfering signal** can jam reader–tag communication |
| **Kill command abuse** | Some tags have a **kill command** that disables the cheap electronics. An adversary could disable a card holding important medical or financial information |

# PART 2 – PASSIVE RFID TAGS

## 8. Passive Tags
- The **most common** RFID tag. Related to **Near Field Communication (NFC)**.
- Sizes range from a penny to a credit card.
- **No internal power source (no battery).** They get **all their power by electromagnetic induction** (Faraday's law, Lecture 1) from the reader's field.

## 9. How a Passive Tag Responds: Modulated Backscatter (7 steps)
1. The **reader** sends an RF signal through its antenna (typically a **loop antenna**).
2. The **EM field** of the signal **induces a voltage** in the tag's antenna coil, producing a small **current** (electromagnetic induction).
3. The current **charges a capacitor**, which **powers the tag's IC**.
4. The IC sends out its **unique digital identifier** as a series of **1s (high voltage)** and **0s (low voltage)**.
5. The original RF signal goes full circle and is **transmitted back** to the reader, but is first **modified by a transistor**.
6. The **transistor switches the signal on and off** according to the high/low voltages from the IC, **embedding the digital identifier** in the signal. This is the **modulated backscatter signal**.
7. The **reader decodes** the signal and the tag is identified (or not).

## 10. Read Range Theory: Magnetic Field of a Loop Antenna
- **Ampère's law:** a current in a conductor produces a **magnetic field** around it.
- **Field on the axis of a circular loop** with N turns, at point P a distance r from the centre:
  **B_P = µ₀ I N a² / [2 (a² + r²)^(3/2)]**
- Since the coil radius is usually small (**a² ≪ r²**):
  **B_P ≈ µ₀ I N a² / (2 r³)**

| Symbol | Meaning | Unit |
|---|---|---|
| B_P | Magnetic field at P | Wb m⁻² (= tesla) |
| µ₀ | Magnetic constant = 4π × 10⁻⁷ | H m⁻¹ |
| I | Current | A |
| N | Number of turns | — |
| a | Loop radius | m |
| r | Distance from the centre of the loop | m |

- **Key observation:** **B decays as 1/r³**. This rapid fall-off is the **main limit on RFID read range**. The equation is used to design reader sensitivity and range.
- **To increase field strength (and range):** increase **I**, **N**, or **a**.

### 10.1 Definitions
- **Weber (Wb):** the unit of **magnetic flux** (density of magnetic field lines in an area). Defined by Faraday's law: a flux change of 1 Wb per second induces an EMF of **1 V**. SI: **kg m² s⁻² A⁻¹**.
- **Henry (H):** the unit of **inductance**. SI: **kg m² s⁻² A⁻²**.

## 11. Pros & Cons of Passive Tags
- **Cheap:** under 1p per tag, with no batteries. There are infrastructure costs (fixed readers).
- **Simultaneous reading** of a few hundred tags at once.
- **Long shelf life:** replace only if the coil or IC is damaged.
- **Variable range:** LF about 10 cm; HF under 3 m; UHF up to 100 m.

## 12. Active & Semi-Passive Tags
| | Semi-passive | Active |
|---|---|---|
| Power supply | Own battery | Own battery |
| Battery powers | IC and circuitry | IC, circuitry **and a transmitter** |
| Return signal | Backscatter | **Broadcast** a bespoke signal (not backscatter) |
| Benefits | **Quicker response** | **More information, longer range** |
| Cost | Moderate | **Expensive** |
| Uses | — | High-value goods tracked over long distances (cars and parts on production lines) |

# PART 3 – APPLYING EMMIE (Case Study: RFID Tagging in Retail)

## 13. Systematic Review
- A **type of literature review** that collects and **critically analyses multiple studies**.
- **Aims:** find as much relevant literature as possible, and use **explicit methods** to identify what can reliably be concluded.
- **A clear, reproducible strategy:**
  - Specified **databases** searched with **pre-defined search terms**
  - Clear **inclusion/exclusion rules**
  - Explicit, systematic methods
  - Result: **less bias** than standard literature reviews
- **Sidebottom et al. (2017):** a systematic review of **tagging to reduce theft in retail**.

## 14. The EMMIE Framework (Johnson, Tilley & Bowers, 2015)
- Designed specifically for **crime prevention** studies. A **coding system** to distil the **quality and coverage** of systematic reviews.
- Defines **5 dimensions**:

| Letter | Dimension | Question for retail tagging |
|---|---|---|
| **E** | **Effect** | Are tags effective at reducing theft? |
| **M** | **Mechanisms / mediators** | *How* do tags reduce theft? |
| **M** | **Moderators** | Under what *conditions* do tags work better or worse? |
| **I** | **Implementation** | What practical issues arise in deploying tags? |
| **E** | **Economic costs** | What are the costs and returns? |

## 15. Retail Crime Context
- A **worldwide problem**: global cost over **$214 billion** (2014–15).
- **Consequences:** business closures, higher prices.
- About **0.65% of total sales** is spent on loss prevention: store detectives, CCTV, locked displays, reinforced packaging, staff screening, signage, alarms, floor design, RFID tags…
- RFID retail tags are known as **Electronic Article Surveillance (EAS)** tags. Used for apparel, groceries, books, electronics.
  - **Pro:** tagged products **stay on open display** and remain accessible.

## 16. Methodology: Inclusion Criteria
- The study must report an **explicit goal of reducing theft, shrinkage or loss** through security tags.
- It must relate specifically to **retail environments**.
- It must include **at least one quantitative outcome measure** (theft, shrinkage or loss).
- It must report **original research** findings.

## 17. E – Effect
- **8 studies** were selected. **Shrinkage** was typically the outcome measure.
- **Mixed results:** **5 positive**, **2 negative (backfire effect)**, **1 no significant change**.
- **Influencing factors:** hard vs soft tags, staff training and awareness, ease of removal, colour.
- **Backfire effect:** faulty tags, tags not disabled properly and system issues cause **high false-alarm rates**, so **staff become less likely to react** to alarms.

## 18. M – Mechanisms
1. **Increased risk**
   - **Perceived risk:** the offender sees or knows tags are in use and **chooses not to steal**.
   - **Actual risk:** the tag triggers the alarm and staff mobilise to detain the offender.
2. **Reduced rewards**
   - The item is **damaged** if the tag is removed without authorisation (mainly apparel and **ink tags**, but also some RFID tags; not electronics or groceries).
3. **Increased effort**
   - It is harder to remove tagged items from the store. **Ease of tag removal** becomes an **arms race**.

## 19. M – Moderators (5)
1. **Shop setting and staff attitude**
   - Layout: **unalarmed doors** are low-risk exits; some areas allow tag removal; **signage** reminds shoppers that tags are in use.
   - Effectiveness **relies heavily on staff response**.
   - **False alarms** reduce staff confidence and anger legitimate shoppers, so staff wave people through (−). But alarms also remind thieves that tags are in use (+).
2. **Customers (including thieves)**
   - **Casual shoplifters:** opportunistic, amateur, impulsive, unplanned. **High-profile tags plus publicity** work best.
   - **Professional shoplifters:** plan ahead, steal to order for resale, analyse store weaknesses, know which tags can be circumvented. **Short-term and covert tagging** works best, because thieves are not fully aware of the risk, which leads to arrests.
3. **Tag type and strategy**
   - **Hard tags:** conspicuous (overt), signalling increased risk of apprehension.
   - **Soft tags:** inconspicuous (covert). They rely more on staff vigilance; the shoplifter only notices when the alarm sounds.
   - **Selective / fractional tagging:** tag only high-value or frequently stolen goods, or a proportion of goods.
   - **Display:** show only part of an item (e.g. one shoe or glove).
4. **Product**
   - Tag shape shouldn't disrupt how products stack on the shelf.
   - There is little benefit in tagging very low-value goods.
5. **Police and criminal justice system:** effectiveness depends on **arrest, conviction and punishment**.
   - **Speed of arrest:** long waits mean dealing with possibly violent offenders, staff taken off the floor, and no guarantee of conviction.
   - **Offender strategies:** **removing a tag is not illegal, walking out without paying is**. One offender removes the tag and passes the item to an accomplice.
   - **Risk vs reward:** punishment may be "worth the risk".

## 20. I – Implementation
- **Source tagging (by the manufacturer) vs in-store tagging:**
  - **Source:** proper, consistent application and **saves staff time**. But manufacturers use **different tag types and systems**, requiring different systems or supplier unification.
  - **In-store (staff):** tags must be **correctly attached**; they can be too easy or too hard to remove.
- **Correct deactivation at point of sale**, otherwise false alarms.
- **Double-tagging** causes false alarms.
- **One system for all merchandise** is preferable.

## 21. E – Economics
- **Tag cost** varies with tag type and **reusability**.
- **Infrastructure costs:** electronic gates, removal and deactivation equipment.
- **Employee costs:** extra security staff at gates, **training**, time to tag and de-tag.
- **Economic returns:** **reduced shrinkage** and an efficient **stock-checking** tool.
