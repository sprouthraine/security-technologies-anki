## Section A – Opportunity & Problem

### Q1. Why does Ekblom say that equating opportunity with "the situation" is incomplete? Use the open-window example. [3]
**Solution:** Opportunity is an **ecological interaction between situation and offender**. An open window three floors up is only an opportunity to an offender with the **resources** (agility, courage, a ladder) to exploit it. Opportunity also only makes sense relative to a **goal** ("opportunity to do what?").

### Q2. List the elements required for a full characterisation of opportunity. [5]
**Solution**
1. **Perception/affordance:** the offender sees the opportunity and understands how to use it
2. **Goals:** positive (get the money) and hygiene (avoid arrest or injury)
3. **Resources/capability** to exploit possibilities and cope with hazards
4. **Presence/access**, including telepresence (internet, drones)
5. **Actions/scripts** to achieve the goals
6. **Dynamics:** encountering, seeking or creating opportunities

### Q3. Define "problem" and "problem solving" according to the definitions in the lecture. [2]
**Solution**
- **Problem:** "the difference between a goal state and a current state" (Jonassen 2000). It is what stops exploiting an opportunity from being child's play.
- **Problem solving:** "the generation and selection of discretionary actions to bring about a goal state" (Scandura 1977).

### Q4. Explain how opportunity and problem relate in an arms race. [2]
**Solution:** One party's opportunity is the other's problem. Each move (e.g. a new lock) sets a problem for the opponent, who develops a countermove (an "adjacent opportunity"). That in turn becomes a problem for the first party, and so on.

### Q5. Distinguish positive goals from hygiene goals, with an example. [2]
**Solution:** **Positive goal:** what the offender wants to achieve (steal a car). **Hygiene goal:** what they want to avoid while doing it (being caught or hurt).

## Section B – Technology & Interventions

### Q6. Give an example of technology being (a) part of the opportunity, (b) part of the problem, (c) able to halt an attack, (d) able to mitigate harm. [4]
**Solution**
- (a) **CCTV** misused to spy on changing rooms, or a wheelie bin used as a leg-up
- (b) **Car alarms**, which are a problem for offenders
- (c) **Personal attack alarms**
- (d) **Backing up phone data** in case the phone is stolen

### Q7. State the three ways of reducing the risk of a crime type, with examples. [3]
**Solution**
1. **Eliminate** the possibility: designing out car tax discs eliminates their forgery
2. **Reduce the likelihood**
3. **Reduce the harm**, during the crime (injury in a robbery) or after it (identity theft from a stolen wallet)

### Q8. Name six civil-world trade-offs that constrain security technology. Why are offenders less constrained? [4]
**Solution:** Any six of: convenience, social inclusivity, market freedom, sustainability, freedom of movement, aesthetics, health and safety, privacy, trust and collective efficacy. Offenders don't have to respect laws, regulations, ethics or public acceptability, so they can adopt technologies freely.

## Section C – Script Clashes

### Q9. What is a crime script, and what is a script clash? Give four examples of clashes. [5]
**Solution**
- **Crime script** (Cornish): the procedure an offender follows to prepare for and commit a crime. Preventers have scripts too.
- **Script clash:** the tactical confrontation between offender and preventer scripts.
- **Examples:** wield force v resist; conceal intent v detect; snoop v maintain privacy; take v keep target; surveil v conceal; pursue v escape; surprise/ambush v be alert; trap v elude; challenge suspect v give plausible response.

### Q10. Why does Ekblom say we "must design things to advantage the good side"? [2]
**Solution:** Script clashes are **generic and perennial**. Technological breakthroughs can **disrupt their balance** and favour whichever side adopts them first, so designers should deliberately bias the outcome towards preventers.

### Q11. Explain the "smart discriminator" problem with an example. [2]
**Solution:** What makes a product good for legitimate users (smaller, lighter, portable, cheap, easy to use) also makes it good for thieves (concealable, removable). The design challenge is to **serve users while thwarting abusers**: **user-friendly but abuser-unfriendly**. Example: swing-down fire escapes are accessible for escape but not for climbing up.

## Section D – Evolution & Arms Races

### Q12. Give two examples of large-scale ("cultural-phylogenetic") change that created new crime opportunities. [2]
**Solution:** The **Neolithic Revolution** (farming, denser populations, stealable property); **mass transportation** (Felson & Eckert); **ICT and generative/agentic AI** today.

### Q13. Give three examples of crime/security co-evolution (arms races). [3]
**Solution:** **Safes** (Neil Shover), **car theft** (Rick Brown), **banknote security**, **malware**, tax evasion, **ransomware-as-a-service**, rogue AI.

### Q14. How has the spread of criminal know-how accelerated? [2]
**Solution:** From pubs and prisons, to the **internet**, guidance documents and **video tips**, to **crime-as-a-service** (e.g. Ransomware-as-a-Service) and AI agents.

### Q15. List six elements of the "gearing up against crime" strategy. [6]
**Solution:** Encourage **variety**; build capacity to **out-innovate** offenders; design to **performance standards** and generic principles; **study offender resources**; **exploit new technology**; **avoid rigidity and lock-in**; **future-proof**; **pipelines of innovation**; **prepare for disruptive change** (quantum crypto); **learn from other evolutionary struggles**.

### Q16. Why does "criminals love standardisation" matter for prevention? [2]
**Solution:** If every lock on an estate is the same design, defeating one defeats them all. **Variety** limits the spread of a single attack method.

### Q17. Name four "other evolutionary struggles" and one lesson from one of them. [3]
**Solution**
- **Struggles:** military/hybrid warfare; predator–prey; pest–farmer; bacteria–antibiotic; immune system–virus.
- **Lesson:** gazelles rely on **remote sensing** (vision) and integrate multiple senses to avoid **false alarms**, rather than waiting for a "burglar alarm" when attacked. Pest and antibiotic battles show that "wonder solutions" are **impermanent**.

### Q18. What is TRIZ, and how can it help crime prevention? [4]
**Solution:** A theory of inventive problem solving derived from analysing many patents. It offers **40 inventive principles** and **39 contradiction parameters**, with lookup tables of which principles solved which contradictions, plus **evolutionary trends** (solid → segmented → flexible → field). It helps resolve the **fundamental contradiction of crime prevention: user-friendly v abuser-unfriendly**.

## Section E – Limitations of Technology

### Q19. Give four limitations of technological approaches to security. [4]
**Solution**
- Self-defeating clunky systems exceed the **compliance budget**.
- Technology can make things worse: **false alarms** waste police time.
- **"Bolt-on, drop-off"** single fixed lines of defence.
- **Solution-driven** approaches lock in responses (e.g. the CCTV rush with white-elephant running costs).
- **Simplistic techno-determinism**, ignoring complex adaptive systems.

### Q20. Ekblom argues these are not inherent limitations. What kinds of technology do they apply to? [5]
**Solution:** Technology that is **poorly designed**, **over-relied on in isolation**, **incorporated too late**, **rigid**, or **incapable of adaptation** (no material or software upgrades) as risks change.

## Section F – Thinking Thief & Design Against Crime

### Q21. Explain the wheelie bin example of "failure to think thief" and the design response. [3]
**Solution:** Wheelie bins, designed for rubbish, give burglars a **leg-up** over fences and walls. A trapping lid was rejected because it would endanger children. The **No ClimBIN** (Jenny Loqvist, 2008) was the design response from a Design Out Crime competition.

### Q22. What does CRAVED stand for, and what is it used for? [3]
**Solution:** **Concealable, Removable, Available, Valuable, Enjoyable, Disposable.** It identifies **hot products**: the risk factors that make items attractive to steal.

### Q23. What is a "crime harvest"? [2]
**Solution:** A wave of crime generated by a new product that has not been designed against crime (Ken Pease). Example: "What made Queen Victoria go red?", the stamp redesign.

### Q24. Define Design Against Crime and list five areas of its scope. [4]
**Solution**
- **DAC** uses the tools, processes and products of design, in partnership with agencies, companies and communities, to **prevent all kinds of crime** and **promote quality of life** through designs that are fit for purpose and contextually appropriate.
- **Scope:** secure products, security products, security components, security features, secure systems, secure information, security communication/art, secure clothing, secure places/environments, secure communities.

### Q25. Is design a product or a process? Explain "reframing". [3]
**Solution:** **Both.** SCP and CPTED practitioners tend to see the end product, but design is fundamentally a **process and a way of thinking**. It covers **problem-finding and problem formulation** as well as problem-solving. **Reframing** means redefining the problem itself (e.g. the CT litter bin, UT Sydney).

### Q26. State the four elements of the Security Function Framework and apply them to the Stop Thief Chair. [8]
**Solution**
| SFF element | Question | Stop Thief Chair |
|---|---|---|
| **Purpose** | What crime problem does it address? | Mainly a functional chair; subsidiary purpose is reducing **bag theft** in bars and restaurants. Desire requirements: stylish, economical, protects venue reputation. Hygiene requirements: safe, sustainable |
| **Niche** | Where does it fit in the security ecosystem? | A **securing product** |
| **Mechanism** | How does it prevent crime? | **Physical anchorage** of the bag (easier for the user to release); **mobilises people** as preventers (surveillance and reaction); keeps the bag in the user's **personal defensible space**; **deters** by raising perceived risk |
| **Technology** | How is it realised? | A **twin notch** in the seat's leading edge. The bag handle sits over it, held between the seat and the back of the user's knees |

### Q27. Give one example of a "hard" and one of a "soft" approach to a crime/disorder problem, and why soft can be better. [3]
**Solution:** **Hard:** target-hardening, aggressive defence. **Soft:** the **Victoria Centre, Nottingham** used an uncomfortable rail, a sloped floor and hot lights so youths moved on without noticing they were being manipulated. Soft approaches avoid **provoking retaliation** and may last longer (the reed and the oak).
