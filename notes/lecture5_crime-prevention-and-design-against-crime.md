# PART 1 – OPPORTUNITY, SCRIPT CLASHES & GEARING UP

## 1. Opportunity, in Depth
- In **Situational Crime Prevention (SCP)**, opportunity is usually equated with the environment or situation. Ekblom argues this is **incomplete**.
- **Opportunity is an ecological interaction between situation and offender.**
  - Example: an open window three floors up is only an opportunity to an offender who has the agility, courage or a **ladder**.
- **A full characterisation of opportunity requires:**
  - **Perception/affordance:** can the offender see the opportunity and understand how to use it?
  - **Goals:** opportunity to achieve *what*, whilst avoiding *what*?
    - **Positive goals** (get the money) and **hygiene goals** (avoid arrest or injury)
    - The **Rational Choice** factors of *risk, effort and reward* all relate to goal states. Risk follows **ISO 31000**: risk = effect of uncertainty on objectives.
  - **Resources/capability** to exploit possibilities and cope with hazards
  - **Presence or access**, including **telepresence** (internet, drones)
  - **Actions** to achieve goals, i.e. **scripts**
  - **Dynamics:** encountering, seeking or creating opportunities
- **Definition:** opportunity is how agents encounter, seek or create circumstances in which their resources let them cope with hazards and exploit possibilities to achieve their goals.

## 2. Problem
- Crime science tends to see problems as something for the **security side** to solve. Wider engineering sees "problem" more broadly, for any agent.
- **Problem = "the difference between a goal state and a current state"** (Jonassen 2000).
- **Problem solving = "the generation and selection of discretionary actions to bring about a goal state"** (Scandura 1977). See Borrion et al. (2020).
- A problem is the set of circumstances that **hinders an agent, with given resources, from immediately achieving a goal**. It is "what stops the exploitation of an opportunity from being child's play" (Ekblom 2016): **purpose that is frustrated**.

## 3. Opportunity ↔ Problem
- **Flip perspectives:** one party's opportunity is another's (or the state's) problem.
- Useful in:
  - **Conflicts:** problems and opportunities are entangled.
  - **Arms races:** each move sets a problem for the other side, who comes up with a countermove (an "adjacent opportunity"), and so on.

## 4. Technology, Opportunity & Problem
- **Technology:** principles derived from natural phenomena, realised as applications and products, to meet a criminal or security goal (adapted from **W. Brian Arthur**).
- Technology can be **part of the opportunity and/or part of the problem**, and can start a fresh opportunity–problem circle.
  - **CCTV:** monitors misbehaviour, but can also be used to spy on changing rooms.
  - **Car alarms:** stopping them going off as intended is a problem for offenders; false alarms are a problem for owners, neighbours and police.
- Technology can:
  - Help either side adapt to conditions (e.g. **street lighting** can help or hinder either side)
  - **Halt** an attack that has been launched (e.g. **personal attack alarms**)
  - **Mitigate** consequences (e.g. **backing up data** in case a phone is stolen)
  - (Even a lockpickers' T-shirt is for sale.)

## 5. Solutions – Interventions
- **Preventive interventions:** reduce crime risk by **blocking, weakening or deflecting causes**, or equivalently by frustrating offenders' goals and plans.
- **Reducing risk of a crime type =**
  1. **Eliminating** the possibility (e.g. designing out car tax discs eliminates their forgery)
  2. **Reducing the likelihood**
  3. **Reducing the harm**, both **during** the crime (e.g. injury in a robbery) and **after** it (e.g. identity theft from a stolen wallet)
- **Strategic actions (speaker notes):**
  - **Anticipate:** write **crime impact statements** for new products. Could it become a target, a tool, or an environment for crime?
  - Where anticipation fails, set up a **learning path** that feeds information on new crimes back to designers, planners and insurers.
  - **Encourage variety.** Criminals love standardisation: defeat one lock and you defeat them all.
  - **Design to performance standards**, kept under review and based on research.
  - **Exploit new technology for prevention** (e.g. hidden codes in fabric prints to trace pirate copies).
  - **Avoid rigidity** ("remember the dinosaurs"): don't get locked into obsolete, non-upgradable systems.

## 6. Challenge 1 for Technological Interventions: Civil-World Trade-offs
- Design contradictions hold back the security side. **Offenders are less constrained.**
- **Security versus:**
  - Convenience
  - Social inclusivity
  - Market freedom
  - Sustainability
  - Freedom of movement
  - Aesthetics
  - Health & safety
  - Privacy
  - Trust & collective efficacy
- Key questions: will innovations relax, bypass or tighten these contradictions? Can we steer designs beneficially, or prepare mitigations for the downsides?

## 7. Challenge 2: Script Clashes
- **Crime scripts** (Derek Cornish): procedures for preparing for and committing crime. **Both offenders and preventers have scripts**, so they meet in **tactical script clashes**.
- **Generic, perennial clashes (offender vs preventer):**

| Offender | vs | Preventer |
|---|---|---|
| Wield force | v | Resist (damage v protect; injure v keep intact) |
| Conceal criminal intent | v | Detect |
| Conceal traces and tracks | v | Detect them |
| Challenge suspect | v | Give plausible response |
| Snoop | v | Maintain privacy |
| Act at will | v | Control misbehaviour |
| Take | v | Keep target asset |
| Confront | v | Avoid |
| Surprise / ambush | v | Be alert |
| Trap | v | Elude |
| Surveil | v | Conceal |
| Pursue | v | Escape |

- These clashes influence criminal plans and outcomes, and they will **always** need to be faced.
- Technology can **disrupt the balance** and favour one side. So ask which side gains from a breakthrough, and **design things to advantage the good side**.

## 8. Tweaking Trade-offs & Biasing Clashes: Desirable Tech Trends
- **Creative leap rather than compromise:** e.g. the internal combustion engine enabled **both** armour **and** mobility.
- **Tunability** of materials and applications to diverse contexts, because "what works" is **context-dependent**.
- **Smart discriminator functions:** what's good for legitimate users (smaller, lighter, portable, durable, cheap, easy) is also good for thieves. How do you serve one while thwarting the other?
- **Adaptable, reconfigurable form:** e.g. swing-down fire escapes are both configurable and discriminating.

## 9. Evolutionary Dynamics (Longer Timescales)
- Technology changes over tactical, strategic and evolutionary scales:
  - **Godfrey-Smith:** "cultural-phylogenetic changes", e.g. the **Neolithic Revolution** (farming, population density, stealable property).
  - **Felson & Eckert** (*Crime in Everyday Life*): **mass transportation** changed routine activities and created many crime opportunities.
  - **ICT** is driving a major phase change today; **generative and agentic AI / LLMs** are the latest.
- Script clashes can **flip in favour of offenders**. What works now may not work in future (**obsolescence**).

## 10. Co-evolution & Arms Races
- Offenders and preventers **co-evolve**, and arms races accelerate and focus change.
  - Examples: banknote security, tax evasion, car theft (Rick Brown), malware, script kiddies, rogue AI.
  - **Classic case: the safe** (Neil Shover).
- **Spread of criminal know-how** has accelerated: from pubs and prisons to the internet, from guidance documents to video tips, to **crime-as-a-service** (e.g. **Ransomware-as-a-Service**) and AI agents.

## 11. Gearing Up Against Crime: A Dynamic Strategy for Arms Races
"No point winning individual battles if we lose the whole campaign."
- **Encourage variety**
- **Motivate, develop and disseminate the capacity to out-innovate offenders**
- **Design to performance standards and generic principles** (design freedom)
- **Study offender resources**, current and future
- **Exploit new technology** for prevention
- **Avoid rigidity and lock-in**
- **Future-proofing**
- **Pipelines of innovation**
- **Prepare for disruptive change**, e.g. quantum cryptography and code-cracking
- **Learn from other evolutionary struggles**

## 12. Learning from Other Struggles
- **Military / hybrid warfare:** stealth vs sensors, countermeasures vs counter-countermeasures ("radar detector detector detector").
- **Predator–prey:** gazelles use **remote sensing** (vision evolved more than 60 times), not "burglar alarms". They also integrate multiple senses to avoid **false alarms**.
- **Pest–farmer** and **bacteria–antibiotic:** "wonder solutions" are impermanent.
- **Immune system–virus:** inspired **artificial immune systems** against computer viruses (IBM).
- **Lessons:** engineering trade-offs (weight v mobility), new design-against-crime strategies, and how to run or avoid arms races.
- **Hard v soft approaches:** hard isn't always best. Example: the Victoria Centre, Nottingham, used an uncomfortable rail, a sloped floor and hot lights to move youths on without provoking them. (Aesop: the reed and the oak.)

## 13. Evolving Faster: TRIZ
- **TRIZ:** a theory of inventive problem solving, based on analysis of many patents.
  - **40 Inventive Principles**
  - **39 contradiction parameters.** The more sharply a contradiction is expressed, the easier it is to solve. This links to the **fundamental contradiction of crime prevention: user-friendly but abuser-unfriendly**.
  - **Lookup tables:** which principles solved which contradictions in the past.
  - **Evolutionary trends of invention:** solid → segmented → flexible → field. These limit the search for the next solution.
- Big question: how will generative AI and LLMs affect the crime/security arms race?

## 14. Limitations of Technological Approaches
- **Self-defeating:** clunky systems overload memory and exceed employees' **compliance budget**.
- **Can make things worse:** e.g. **false burglar alarms** waste police time and annoy neighbours.
- **"Bolt-on, drop-off" solutions** give a single fixed line of defence.
- **Solution- or supply-driven** approaches canalise responses and constrain future adaptability. Example: the rush into public-space **CCTV**, with white-elephant and running-cost legacies.
- **Simplistic techno-determinism:** these are **complex adaptive systems**. Each party adjusts to the other, often with side-effects, so solutions need subtlety and stakeholder understanding.

### But these are not inherent limitations. They apply only to technology that is:
- **Poorly designed** (abuser-unfriendly without also being user-friendly)
- **Over-relied on** in isolation from human and system considerations
- **Incorporated too late** in the design process
- **Rigid** in the face of real-life complexity
- **Not adaptable** to changing risk during its lifetime (no material or software upgrades)

# PART 2 – THINKING THIEF & THINKING DESIGNER

## 15. The Right Mindset
- **Wrong mindset = failure to "think thief".**
  - **Wheelie bin:** "a receptacle for grime, or a tool for crime?" Burglars use it as a **leg-up** over fences. A trapping lid was rejected because it would endanger children.
  - **Design response:** the **No ClimBIN** (Jenny Loqvist, Griffith University, 2008), from a Design Out Crime competition (Curtin University).
- **Failure to "think drug user":** phone boxes with sides obscured by adverts became convenient places to inject drugs, one of them under an advert for a drug-help charity.

## 16. Crime Harvests & Hot Products
- **Crime harvests** (Ken Pease): a new product generates a wave of crime. Example: *"What made Queen Victoria go red?"* (Likely referring to the Penny Black stamp: its red cancellation mark could be cleaned off and the stamp reused, so it was replaced by the Penny Red with a black cancellation. The slide is image-only, so this is an interpretation.)
- **Hot products: risk factors for theft (CRAVED)**
  - **C**oncealable
  - **R**emovable
  - **A**vailable
  - **V**aluable
  - **E**njoyable
  - **D**isposable
- **But beware obsession:** "paranoid products" are over-secured designs.

## 17. Design Against Crime (DAC)
- Uses the **tools, processes and products of design**, in partnership with agencies, companies and communities, to:
  - **Prevent all kinds of crime**, including antisocial behaviour, drug abuse/dealing and terrorism
  - **Promote quality of life and sustainable living** through community safety
  - Through designs that are **fit for purpose and contextually appropriate** in all other respects
- **Aims:** equip designers to **think thief** and crime-prevention experts to **draw on design**. DAC can also be quirky or fun.

### 17.1 Scope of DAC (with examples)
| Category | Example |
|---|---|
| Secure products | Inherently secure product; **Vexed Generation / Puma bike** |
| Evolution of a secure product | **Milled vs hammered coins**, preventing coin clipping |
| Security products | **Clothes tagging device** |
| Security communication | Signage / awareness products |
| Security components | **Brand protection** features |
| Security systems | **Chips protecting high-value alpacas** |
| Security communication/art | Public art carrying security messages |
| Secure clothing | Wearable solutions: jacket camera, anti-theft underarm bag |
| Secure places / environments | **Maiden Castle** (Iron Age hill fort with tortuous entrances); **Sandy Hook School** redesign |
| Secure communities | Collective efficacy |
- **Hi-tech vs lo-tech** solutions can both work.
- **Design is not just products but processes.**

## 18. Design: Product or Process?
- SCP and **CPTED** (Crime Prevention Through Environmental Design) practitioners view design as **end products** (secure laptops, safe buildings).
- The more fundamental view: the **design process and design thinking**.
- Design covers **problem-solving** and also **problem formulation / problem-finding**, which designers call **reframing**.
  - Example: the **CT litter bin** (Designing Out Crime Research Centre, UT Sydney) reframed the problem.

## 19. Security Function Framework (SFF)
A way of **describing designed interventions** so that knowledge can be shared:

| Dimension | Question |
|---|---|
| **Purpose** | What crime problem(s) does the design address? |
| **Niche** | How does the design fit in the security ecosystem? |
| **Mechanism** | How does the design work in preventing crime? |
| **Technology / technicality** | How is it realised through materials, construction and operation? |

## 20. Case Study: The Stop Thief Chair (SFF applied)
- **Purpose:**
  - Principal: a fully functional chair
  - Subsidiary: reduce **theft of customers' bags** in bars and restaurants
  - Desire requirements: stylish, economical, protects the venue's reputation as safe and caring
  - Hygiene requirements: physically safe, sustainable
- **Niche:** a **securing product**.
- **Mechanism:**
  - **Physical anchorage** of the bag, which is differentially easier for the user to release
  - **Mobilises people** as crime preventers, giving surveillance and reaction
  - Protects the bag within the user's **personal defensible space**
  - **Deters** offenders by raising the **perceived risk** of being caught
- **Technicality:** a **twin notch** cut into the leading edge of the seat. The bag handle sits over the notch and is held between the seat and the back of the user's knees.
